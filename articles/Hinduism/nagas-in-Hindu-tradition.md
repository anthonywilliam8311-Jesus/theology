Few figures in Hindu tradition are as layered as the Naga. A Naga can be a cosmic being who carries the universe, a royal figure ruling a jewelled kingdom, a village guardian beside a pond, or a symbol of the energy coiled within the human body. To see only a "snake" in these stories is to miss most of what they say to the Hindu mind. 

However, for the Christian, the veneration of serpents raises profound theological and spiritual questions. The biblical narrative presents the serpent not as a cosmic supporter of the universe or a symbol of hidden wisdom, but as the ancient deceiver. When we examine how Nagas are worshipped in Hinduism, we are confronted with a worldview that fundamentally differs from the biblical revelation of God, creation, and salvation.

This article follows the Nagas from their genealogy in the epics, through the realities of how they are worshipped today, to the theological divide between the Hindu veneration of the serpent and the Christian confession of Christ's victory over it.

## The Biblical Context of the Serpent
Before examining Hindu practice, it is necessary to establish the biblical framework. In Scripture, the serpent is introduced in Genesis as the most cunning of creatures, who deceives Eve and brings sin into the world. 

"Now the serpent was more crafty than any other beast of the field that the LORD God had made." — Genesis 3:1

The New Testament identifies the spiritual reality behind this Edenic serpent:
"And the great dragon was thrown down, that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world." — Revelation 12:9

While Hindu tradition often views the serpent as a neutral or even divine force of nature, wisdom, and cosmic order, the biblical tradition views the serpent as a created being that rebelled against its Creator. This foundational difference shapes how each tradition understands the veneration of serpents.

## What Is a Naga?
In Sanskrit, *nāga* (नाग) means "snake," most often the cobra. In the texts, it also names a race of semi-divine beings. Textual descriptions agree on several defining traits:
* **Shape-shifting**: A Naga may appear as a full serpent, as a human, or as a hybrid.
* **Wealth and splendor**: Their realm is filled with gems, gold, and treasure.
* **Power over water**: Rivers, lakes, and wells fall under their care.
* **Moral ambiguity**: Nagas can be generous benefactors or fierce enemies.

Female Nagas are called *Naginis*. From a Christian perspective, the attribution of divine or semi-divine status to these creatures reflects the biblical warning against exchanging the truth of God for a lie and worshiping the creature rather than the Creator (Romans 1:25).

## Origins: The Family of Kashyapa
The *Mahabharata* and the *Puranas* trace the Nagas to the sage Kashyapa and his wife Kadru. 

### The Wager That Divided Two Families
The *Adi Parva* of the *Mahabharata* explains why the Nagas and Garuda became enemies. The sisters Kadru and Vinata made a bet over the colour of the tail of a divine horse. To win, Kadru asked her serpent sons to coil around the horse's tail. Some refused, and she cursed them to perish in a future snake sacrifice.

Notice what the story establishes: The Nagas are caught in a family quarrel, and a mother's curse hangs over them. In contrast, the biblical narrative traces the curse of the serpent not to a family dispute among demigods, but to a direct judgment by the one sovereign God against a rebellious creature for its deception of humanity (Genesis 3:14).

## The Great Nagas and the Hindu Divine
In Hinduism, the supreme deities are intimately associated with serpent power. This is a major point of theological divergence from Christianity, where God is entirely holy and distinct from His creation.

### Shesha (Ananta): The Endless One
Shesha is the cosmic serpent on whose coils Vishnu rests on the ocean of milk. 
> "Among the Nagas, I am Ananta." — Bhagavad Gita 10.29

In Vaishnavism, the supreme sustainer of the universe relies upon the serpent. The Christian apologetic response is that the biblical God does not rest upon created beings; He upholds the universe by the word of His power (Hebrews 1:3).

### Vasuki and the Churning of the Ocean
Vasuki is the best-known Naga king, used as the rope to churn the ocean of milk. He is also identified with the serpent coiled around Shiva's neck. 
> "Among serpents, I am Vasuki." — Bhagavad Gita 10.28

Here, the god of destruction wears the serpent as an ornament, symbolizing mastery over fear and death. Christianity teaches that mastery over death is not found in wearing the serpent as an ornament, but in the cross of Christ, who conquered death through His resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:54-57).

### Takshaka and the Snake Sacrifice
Takshaka is the Naga who kills King Parikshit. Parikshit's son Janamejaya began the *Sarpa Satra*, a vast sacrifice intended to destroy all serpents. The sacrifice was only stopped by the young Astika. 

The story raises an enduring question: Is it just to destroy an entire people for the act of one? The biblical answer is that God is the righteous judge of all the earth, and He does not require the blood of creatures to satisfy His justice; rather, He provides the ultimate sacrifice Himself in Christ.

## How Nagas Are Worshipped Today
To understand the spiritual reality of Naga worship, one must look at how it is practiced by millions of Hindus today. From a Christian apologetic perspective, these practices are not merely cultural folklore; they engage with spiritual realities that Scripture warns against.

### Naga Panchami
Naga Panchami, celebrated in the monsoon month of Shravana, is the best-known festival of serpent worship. Devotees offer milk, flowers, and prayers at snake images, live cobras, and anthills, seeking protection for their families. 

The theological implication here is one of appeasement. Devotees offer gifts to the serpent to ward off its venom and secure its favor. The biblical perspective asks: Why must humanity appease a created creature? In Christianity, God is the one who provides protection, and He invites His children to come to Him freely through grace, not through the appeasement of nature spirits (Matthew 6:25-33).

### Sacred Groves and Shrines
In Kerala, family estates traditionally keep *sarpa kavus*, small groves set aside for serpents. In Karnataka, Kukke Subramanya is a major pilgrimage site where Naga worship is central. Stones carved with entwined serpents (*nagakals*) are placed beneath sacred trees.

The Apostle Paul addresses this directly in his letter to the Corinthians:
"What do I imply? That an idol is anything, or that food offered to idols is anything? No, I imply that what pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God." — 1 Corinthians 10:19-20

From a Christian worldview, the veneration of shrines dedicated to serpent spirits is not directed toward an inert stone or a literal snake, but engages with spiritual forces of evil that oppose the knowledge of God.

### Kundalini: The Serpent Within
In later yogic and Tantric thought, *Kundalini* is described as a coiled serpent power resting at the base of the spine, awakened through practice and rising through the body's energy centers. 

This is perhaps the most profound theological divergence. Hinduism seeks spiritual awakening and liberation (Moksha) by arousing the "serpent power" within. Christianity teaches that the human heart is spiritually dead in sin and cannot be awakened by self-generated, esoteric power. Spiritual awakening comes only through the Holy Spirit, who regenerates the believer through faith in Christ (Ephesians 2:1-5). To seek spiritual power through the symbolism and practices of the serpent is to seek life from the very symbol of humanity's fall.

## The Theological Divide: Christ and the Serpent
When we compare the Hindu veneration of the Naga with the Christian Gospel, we see two entirely different answers to the problem of evil and the human condition.

### The Protoevangelium
Immediately after the fall in Eden, God speaks to the serpent:
"I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring; he shall bruise your head, and you shall strike his heel." — Genesis 3:15

This is the *Protoevangelium*, the first gospel. It promises that a descendant of the woman will ultimately crush the head of the serpent. 

### Krishna vs. Christ
It is fascinating to note that Hinduism also features a narrative where a divine figure subdues a serpent. In the *Bhagavata Purana*, the child Krishna wrestles the many-hooded serpent Kaliya and dances on his hoods. 

However, the theological meaning is entirely different. Krishna subdues Kaliya to cleanse a polluted river and assert divine dominance over a local nature spirit. Christ, on the other hand, crushes the head of the ancient serpent (Satan) to cleanse humanity from the pollution of sin and break the power of death. 
"He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him." — Colossians 2:15

Krishna dances on the serpent to show mastery; Christ dies on the cross to show love and achieve redemption. 

## What Christians Do Not Mean
When Christians critique the worship of Nagas, it is important to clarify what the Christian position is not:
* Christians do not believe that Hindus are merely worshiping literal, physical snakes.
* Christians do not deny that serpents play a significant cultural and historical role in Indian tradition.
* Christians do not believe that Hindus are inherently more evil or sinful than anyone else; all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God (Romans 3:23).

Instead, the Christian critique is theological and spiritual: The veneration of the Naga is a misdirection of the worship that belongs to God alone, and it engages with spiritual forces that are ultimately opposed to the revelation of Jesus Christ.

## The Christian Position in Summary
* The Naga is a created being, part of the natural world, and not a divine entity to be worshipped or appeased.
* Serpent worship, including Naga Panchami and Kundalini yoga, falls under the biblical prohibition of idolatry and the worship of created things (Romans 1:25).
* The biblical narrative identifies the serpent as the ancient deceiver, Satan, whose head was crushed by Jesus Christ.
* Spiritual awakening and liberation from sin are not found in awakening a "serpent power" within, but through the regenerating work of the Holy Spirit.
* Christians are called to love their Hindu neighbors, respect their culture, and gently share the true freedom found in Christ, who delivers us from the fear of death and spiritual powers.

## Conclusion
The Hindu veneration of the Naga should not be dismissed as mere superstition; it is a deeply embedded theological system that seeks to make sense of nature, power, and the divine. However, when viewed through the lens of biblical revelation, it represents a profound spiritual tragedy. 

Instead of worshiping the Creator, who is blessed forever, Hindu tradition has woven the symbol of the deceiver into the very fabric of its cosmic order, resting its supreme gods upon the coils of the serpent and seeking inner awakening through its power.

The New Testament presents Jesus Christ as the Word made flesh, the Creator of all things, who came into the world to destroy the works of the devil (1 John 3:8). 

Not all of these theological differences are merely academic; they represent two competing claims about the nature of reality, the identity of God, and the destiny of the human soul. Ultimately, the question of the Naga brings us back to the central Christian question: 

Who is the serpent, and who is the Savior?

If the serpent in Eden was the deceiver who brought death, and if Jesus Christ is the seed of the woman who crushed the serpent's head on the cross, then the veneration of the Naga is fundamentally a rejection of the victory Christ has already won. This is why the Gospel calls humanity to turn away from the worship of created things—including the serpent—and to place their faith in the one true God and His Son, Jesus Christ.
