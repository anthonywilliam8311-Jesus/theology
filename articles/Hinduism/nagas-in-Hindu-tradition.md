Few figures in Hindu tradition are as layered as the Naga. To the Hindu mind, the Naga is a cosmic being, a guardian of hidden wealth, and a symbol of divine energy coiled within the human body. However, for the Christian, the veneration of serpents raises profound theological and spiritual questions. The biblical narrative presents the serpent not as a cosmic supporter of the universe, but as the ancient deceiver. When we examine how Nagas are worshipped in Hinduism, we are confronted with a worldview that fundamentally differs from the biblical revelation of God, creation, and salvation.

## The Biblical Context of the Serpent

Before examining Hindu practice, it is necessary to establish the biblical framework. In Scripture, the serpent is introduced in Genesis as the most cunning of creatures, who deceives Eve and brings sin and death into the world. 

> "Now the serpent was more crafty than any other beast of the field that the LORD God had made." — Genesis 3:1

The New Testament explicitly identifies the spiritual reality behind this Edenic serpent, removing any ambiguity about its true nature:

> "And the great dragon was thrown down, that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world." — Revelation 12:9

While Hindu tradition often views the serpent as a neutral or even divine force of nature and wisdom, the biblical tradition views the serpent as a created being that rebelled against its Creator. This foundational difference shapes how each tradition understands the veneration of serpents.

## How Nagas Are Worshipped in Hinduism

To understand the spiritual reality of Naga worship, one must look at how it is practiced by millions of Hindus today. From a Christian apologetic perspective, these practices are not merely cultural folklore; they engage with spiritual realities that Scripture explicitly warns against.

- **Naga Panchami**: Celebrated in the monsoon month of Shravana, this is the most famous festival of serpent worship. Devotees offer milk, flowers, and prayers to live cobras, snake images, and anthills, seeking to appease the serpent and ward off venomous bites.
- **Sacred Groves (Sarpa Kavus)**: In regions like Kerala, families maintain untouched groves dedicated to serpent spirits. Major pilgrimage sites, such as Kukke Subramanya in Karnataka, center entirely around Naga worship, where carved stones (*nagakals*) are venerated beneath sacred trees.
- **Kundalini Yoga**: In Tantric thought, *Kundalini* is described as a coiled serpent power resting at the base of the spine. Spiritual awakening is sought by "raising" this serpent energy through the body's chakras.

The theological implication of these practices is one of appeasement and idolatry. Devotees offer gifts to the serpent to secure its favor. The biblical perspective asks: Why must humanity appease a created creature? The Apostle Paul addresses this directly:

> "What do I imply? That an idol is anything, or that food offered to idols is anything? No, I imply that what pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God." — 1 Corinthians 10:19-20

From a Christian worldview, the veneration of shrines dedicated to serpent spirits is not directed toward an inert stone or a literal snake, but engages with spiritual forces that oppose the knowledge of the one true God.

## The Theological Divide: Christ and the Serpent

When we compare the Hindu veneration of the Naga with the Christian Gospel, we see two entirely different answers to the problem of evil and the human condition. 

Immediately after the fall in Eden, God speaks to the serpent, issuing the *Protoevangelium* (the first gospel):

> "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring; he shall bruise your head, and you shall strike his heel." — Genesis 3:15

It is fascinating to note that Hinduism also features a narrative where a divine figure subdues a serpent. In the *Bhagavata Purana*, the child Krishna wrestles the many-hooded serpent Kaliya and dances on his hoods to cleanse a polluted river. 

However, the theological meaning is entirely different. Krishna subdues Kaliya to assert divine dominance over a local nature spirit. Christ, on the other hand, crushes the head of the ancient serpent to cleanse humanity from the pollution of sin and break the power of death forever. 

> "He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him." — Colossians 2:15

Krishna dances on the serpent to show mastery; Christ dies on the cross to show love and achieve redemption.

## What Christians Do Not Mean

When Christians critique the worship of Nagas, it is important to clarify what the Christian position is *not*:

- Christians do not believe that Hindus are merely worshiping literal, physical snakes without spiritual significance.
- Christians do not deny that serpents play a significant cultural and historical role in Indian tradition.
- Christians do not believe that Hindus are inherently more evil than anyone else; all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God (Romans 3:23).

Instead, the Christian critique is theological and spiritual: The veneration of the Naga is a misdirection of the worship that belongs to God alone, and it seeks spiritual life from the very symbol of humanity's fall.

## Conclusion

The Hindu veneration of the Naga should not be dismissed as mere superstition; it is a deeply embedded theological system that seeks to make sense of nature, power, and the divine. However, when viewed through the lens of biblical revelation, it represents a profound spiritual tragedy. 

Instead of worshiping the Creator, who is blessed forever, Hindu tradition has woven the symbol of the deceiver into the very fabric of its cosmic order, resting its supreme gods upon the coils of the serpent and seeking inner awakening through its power.

The New Testament presents Jesus Christ as the Word made flesh, the Creator of all things, who came into the world to destroy the works of the devil (1 John 3:8). Ultimately, the question of the Naga brings us back to the central Christian question: Who is the serpent, and who is the Savior? 

If the serpent in Eden was the deceiver who brought death, and if Jesus Christ is the seed of the woman who crushed the serpent's head on the cross, then the veneration of the Naga is fundamentally a rejection of the victory Christ has already won. The Gospel calls humanity to turn away from the worship of created things and to place their faith in the one true God.
